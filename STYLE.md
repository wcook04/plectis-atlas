# How to write an explanation

Name the reader and the part of the mathematics you explain. The useful test is whether that reader can follow what is being done, why it is needed, and why it is valid.

## State exactly what you cover

An explanation can cover a definition, example, lemma or complete argument. Name that scope at the top. State the relevant hypotheses, quantifiers and conventions. Identify any result you use as an input. Distinguish claims in a manuscript, the steps you have checked, a formal statement comparison, and a replay receipt.

Do not assume that a manuscript's claim has been established just because it is in the catalogue. Do not say an entire family is explained when the piece covers one step.

## Give the reader a reason to follow

Begin with the mathematical question or difficulty this piece resolves. Use an original historical statement when it is available and helpful. A missing original source does not prevent a clearly labelled explanation of a modern statement.

For a substantial argument, state the plan before carrying it out. Spend the most space on the idea that makes the proof work. Explain a nontrivial choice before using it. Routine steps can be concise when the reader has the stated background.

## Use examples with a purpose

Use a numerical example, construction or diagram when it reveals the mechanism or tests a convention. State the limit of the example. A concrete example is not a proof of the general statement. Numerical illustrations are optional for abstract material; do not force them onto every symbol.

## Use standard mathematical language

Define unfamiliar terms before using them. Use established names for established results. Give a symbol one meaning and introduce notation only when it helps the reader. Keep implementation identifiers in source links or a formal-support section unless they are being explained.

Prefer direct sentences. Replace an unsupported 'clearly' with its reason. Use British spelling in atlas documentation, and commas, colons and full stops for prose punctuation. Authors may use normal mathematical notation.

## Locate the evidence

Credit an idea where it is used and cite the exact theorem, page or passage you read. Distinguish an existing citation from an additional source you found. Say 'the earliest source I found' when describing a bounded search.

If formal support matters to the explanation, identify the exact Lean statement and its recorded version. Describe a Comparator acceptance only when a run is evidenced, and say what that run covers. The correspondence with the paper is a separate judgement. If no formal statement is linked to the part you explain, say so without implying that the part is false.

## Keep limitations visible

State the scope and unresolved dependencies of the explanation. Include a stronger open question only if it is relevant and sourced. Use visible text for unchecked steps; HTML comments hide them from readers. An unresolved step essential to the claimed explanation prevents it being marked reviewed.

## Authorship and review

Use human-authored prose and understand each sentence you sign. Visibly marked, disclosed tool-drafted passages are permitted. Explain the reasoning in public discussion yourself. A reviewer checks the mathematics and the fit for the named reader; a short reader response can reveal a missing explanation.

Refer to other people by name or as they/them unless they have stated their pronouns; a name does not tell you them. The same holds for findings recorded in `curated/community.json`, whose checker rejects gendered pronouns and summaries cut off mid-sentence.

Use the template and existing contribution path. Keep figures beside the Markdown file, describe them in the text, and cite their source or generation code. Run the contribution checker. A style warning prompts judgement; it does not automatically override a mathematically appropriate presentation.
